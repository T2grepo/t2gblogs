**Meta description:** A federal lawsuit filed August 31 alleges Amazon quietly built an undisclosed price floor into its "second-price" ad auctions since 2019, extracting an estimated $20B+ from advertisers. Here's what sellers should actually do about ACOS while that plays out.

If you've spent the last two years feeling like your Amazon ad costs were climbing faster than any targeting change could explain, there's now a federal complaint that says you weren't imagining it.

On August 31, the FTC and attorneys general from 22 states sued Amazon in the Western District of Washington, alleging the company built an undisclosed mechanism called a "soft reserve price" into its advertising auctions starting in 2019. Amazon has long told advertisers its Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands auctions run on a "second-price" model — you bid what you're willing to pay, but you're only charged slightly above the next-highest bid. The complaint alleges that underneath that promise, Amazon inserted a proxy floor value it calculated internally, meaning the price advertisers actually paid wasn't the honest output of competition between bidders — it was Amazon's own number, undisclosed, layered on top. The FTC puts the damage at more than $20 billion extracted from roughly 1.2 million advertisers, including over 500,000 small and medium-sized businesses. Amazon has called the suit "misguided" and points to $8 billion in advertiser savings it says its auction systems delivered between 2021 and 2025.

We're not going to litigate who's right here — that's what the Western District of Washington is for. What matters for sellers running campaigns right now is simpler: this is the first time a mechanism this specific, this well-documented, and this systemic has been put on the record as a *possible* explanation for a trend every serious seller has already felt. Amazon's average CPC has climbed roughly 35% since 2023, now sitting around $1.21 across ad types, with mid-teens percentage jumps in competitive categories over the past year alone. Whether or not the soft reserve allegation holds up, the practical reality hasn't changed: CPC inflation is structural, not a targeting problem you can fix by tightening a few keywords, and it isn't going to reverse on its own before this case resolves.

## Why "control your targeting" isn't a full answer anymore

The standard ACOS advice — trim your search terms, raise negative keyword coverage, tighten match types — still matters and you should still do it. But it treats ACOS as purely a function of *your* decisions inside an auction whose rules you fully understand. If a meaningful share of what you're paying is a floor you can't see, no amount of bid discipline on your end changes the floor. That reframes the job: instead of only optimizing against your own campaign data, you need to optimize against the *outcome* — your actual margin after ad spend — because the auction mechanics are, at least partially, out of your hands.

## Five moves that actually move ACOS/TACOS right now

1. **Stop managing ACOS in isolation from TACoS.** If CPC inflation is structural, a campaign can look "efficient" on ACOS while your blended TACoS still creeps up because organic sales aren't growing fast enough to absorb it. Pull both metrics into the same weekly review. If TACoS is rising while ACOS looks flat, your ad spend is increasingly doing work your organic ranking used to do for free — which is a catalog problem, not a bidding problem.

2. **Run a Search Query Performance audit specifically on branded terms.** This report (inside Brand Analytics) shows how often shoppers who searched your own brand name still clicked a competitor or an ad before buying. If you're paying CPC to defend searches you should be winning organically, that's spend you can cut immediately without touching acquisition campaigns.

3. **Switch top-of-search placements from "up and down" to bid-capped dynamic bidding on your worst-margin ASINs.** "Up and down" bidding lets Amazon increase your bid up to 100% in real time when it predicts a conversion — reasonable when you trust the floor is clean, riskier when you can't verify it. On SKUs where margin is already thin, cap the ceiling and watch conversion rate before re-opening it.

4. **Reallocate a fixed percentage of ad budget toward listing and catalog work every month.** If auction costs are inflated and largely out of your control, the lever you *do* fully control is conversion rate once the click lands — which is a function of images, A+ Content, and title/bullet clarity. A 2-point CVR improvement often does more for TACoS than another week of keyword pruning, and it compounds instead of resetting every auction.

5. **Benchmark your CPC trend against category averages, not just your own history.** If your CPCs are rising in line with the ~35% two-year market trend, that's the environment, not a targeting failure — don't panic-cut budget. If they're rising faster than category benchmarks, that's a specific signal to dig into placement mix and bidding strategy rather than assume it's just "Amazon ads getting more expensive."

## What to watch next

Amazon's unBoxed advertiser conference runs September 28–30 in San Francisco, and Amazon Accelerate follows September 22–24 in Seattle — both likely venues for Amazon to address advertiser questions about auction transparency, even indirectly. Discovery in the FTC case will also surface actual auction mechanics over the coming months, which could eventually give sellers a clearer picture of what "second price" really means in practice. Until then, treat elevated CPCs as a market condition to manage around, not a puzzle to solve — the fix is a portfolio of catalog, budget, and placement decisions, not a single bid adjustment.
