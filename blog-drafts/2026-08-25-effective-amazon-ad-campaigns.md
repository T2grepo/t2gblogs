# Your Keyword List Isn't the Bottleneck Anymore: Rebuilding Amazon Campaigns for How Ads Actually Get Served in 2026

For years, "improve your Amazon ads" meant one thing: refine the keyword list. Add negatives, harvest search terms, chase exact match. That's still worth doing, but it's no longer where most of the upside is. Amazon's own ad platform has moved — how campaigns get built, how targeting decisions get made, and where impressions actually come from have all shifted meaningfully in the last few months. Sellers still running campaigns the way they did in 2023 are leaving performance on the table not because their keyword lists are bad, but because the keyword list stopped being the main lever.

Two developments this month make that concrete.

## What Changed: Ads Agent Went Global, and Product Targeting Took the Lead

Amazon Ads has been rolling out **Ads Agent**, its natural-language campaign-builder for Amazon DSP, since earlier this year — you describe a media plan and it builds the campaign and ad group structure for you. This month, Amazon expanded it beyond its initial markets, adding support for 18 languages and 27 dialects and extending it to every region where DSP is available, including a recent UK expansion. Amazon's own reporting on advertisers using the tool shows an average 18% reduction in CPMs and a 16% reduction in CPA, with roughly two-thirds of users seeing measurable delivery improvements. That's not a marginal efficiency tool — it's Amazon telling advertisers, fairly directly, that campaigns built through its own AI structuring are outperforming ones built manually.

At the same time, the balance between keyword and product targeting has shifted. Amazon's matching systems now lean heavily on browsing behavior, purchase history, and on-page signals to decide who sees an ad — not just the literal search term typed into the box. A shopper can land on your Sponsored Products ad without ever searching your exact keyword, because Amazon's models inferred intent from what they've been browsing. That makes the detail page — yours and your competitors' — the place where a growing share of buying decisions actually get contested, which is why product targeting (bidding to appear on competitor and complementary listings) now carries more relative weight than it did two years ago, when keyword match types did most of the work.

Neither of these changes means keyword strategy stopped mattering. It means it stopped being sufficient on its own.

## Five Concrete Changes to Make to Your Campaign Structure

**1. Split product targeting into its own campaigns, not an afterthought inside auto campaigns.** If your product targeting is still riding inside an "Auto" campaign with no bid differentiation, you're treating your most contested placements — competitor detail pages — the same as everything else Amazon's algorithm decides to show you. Build dedicated manual product-targeting campaigns against (a) direct competitor ASINs, (b) complementary products your buyers also purchase, and (c) your own underperforming ASINs to defend detail-page real estate. Bid each category independently; a defensive bid on your own product page and an offensive bid on a competitor's should never share a budget line.

**2. Run an Ads Agent test campaign against one of your existing manually-built campaigns.** With Ads Agent now available across most major markets and languages, this is the moment to A/B it rather than take Amazon's 18%-CPM claim on faith. Pick one product line, build a parallel campaign through Ads Agent with the same budget, and run both for three to four weeks before comparing CPC, CPA, and conversion rate. If the structure it recommends outperforms yours, that's a signal about where Amazon's own targeting models are heading — worth acting on even outside DSP.

**3. Stop treating dayparting as optional.** Amazon still doesn't offer native hour-by-hour bid scheduling inside Seller Central for Sponsored Products the way it does for DSP — you need Rules-Based Bidding (schedule rules) or a third-party tool to do it. Sellers who set this up typically see ACOS drop noticeably within the first month simply by pulling bids back overnight and in low-conversion windows and pushing them up during your category's proven peak hours. If you haven't touched your dayparting rules in the last quarter, they're stale — buyer behavior shifts with seasonality, and a rule set calibrated for spring browsing patterns is the wrong one for late-summer back-to-school or early Q4 traffic.

**4. Refresh creative on a fixed cadence tied to placement performance, not a calendar guess.** With product targeting now competing directly on detail pages, the images and video your ad serves next to a competitor's listing matter more than they did when most impressions came from search results. Pull your placement report quarterly and flag any ASIN where product-page placements convert meaningfully below search placements — that gap is usually a creative problem (a hero image that doesn't hold up next to a competitor's, no comparison chart, no video) more often than a targeting problem.

**5. Rebuild your negative keyword list around match-type drift, not just wasted spend.** Because Amazon is matching ads to inferred intent rather than literal search terms more often now, search term reports increasingly show near-misses that used to be rarer — adjacent-category terms, competitor brand searches that shouldn't convert for you, and generic terms with low buyer intent. Run this audit every 8 days for Sponsored Products and every 15 for Sponsored Brands, and specifically look for the newer pattern: broader, more "inferred" matches sneaking into spend that a stricter negative list a year ago wouldn't have needed to catch.

## The Bigger Shift to Plan Around

None of this is a one-time fix — it's a signal that Amazon's ad platform is moving toward AI-assisted structuring and behavior-based targeting faster than most sellers' campaign habits are adapting. The accounts pulling ahead right now aren't necessarily spending more; they're the ones restructuring product targeting as its own discipline, testing Amazon's own automation against their manual builds instead of ignoring it, and treating creative and dayparting as live inputs that need revisiting every quarter rather than settings from a launch checklist two years ago.

Start with the campaign audit: are your product-targeting and keyword-targeting campaigns actually separated with independent bids, and when did you last touch your dayparting rules or run a real creative refresh? If the honest answer is "a while ago," that's the highest-leverage place to start this week.
